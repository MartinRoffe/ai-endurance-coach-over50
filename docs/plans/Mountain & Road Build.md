## What the Sources Say

Eight findings shaped the plan. Each one opens to show the detail and where it lands in your weeks.

**Train in a fixed order: aerobic base, strength, muscular endurance, then specific work**Uphill AthleteTheir plans rest on three pillars: aerobic base, periodised strength, and muscular endurance sessions with a heavy pack on the steepest hill available.

Steve House states the order directly: aerobic base first, then strength, then muscular endurance, then training specific to the objective as it approaches. Their 24-week mountaineering plan uses the same three blocks and starts with a heart rate test to set zones.

Phases 1 and 2 are base plus strength. Weighted hill sessions wait until January.

**After 50, two hard days a week with 48 to 72 hours between them**Uphill Athlete / Joe FrielThe 5+2 pattern: hard, easy, easy, hard, easy, easy, easy. Two of the easy days can be full rest.

The article argues a third hard session rarely pays off that week and costs you about four weeks later, when accumulated fatigue blunts the sessions that mattered. A missed hard day is moved by 48 to 72 hours, never stacked. In a rough week, dropping to four easy days and one hard day is the intended adjustment. It also recommends building one capacity at a time: strength in October and November, base from December, intensity later.

Tuesday and Saturday are the hard days all 23 weeks. Monday and Friday are rest. Sunday's ride is long but stays easy.

**Muscular endurance is the mountain-specific quality, and it is easy to overdo**Uphill AthleteLegs failing before lungs on a loaded climb is the sign you lack it. It is trained with a weighted pack on steep ground at a leg-burn effort you can still talk through.

Their guidance: start at 5 to 10 percent of body weight if new to it, with many fit climbers working at 10 to 30 percent. Use slopes of 30 percent grade or steeper. Do one session a week for 6 to 10 sessions over six to eight weeks. Progress weight or vertical, never both in the same week. Allow at least three easy days afterwards. Keep all your easy aerobic volume alongside it. They suggest carrying water so it can be tipped out at the top to spare the knees on the way down.

They also set an entry test: your aerobic threshold heart rate should be within 10 percent of your anaerobic threshold before you start. If the gap is wider, more easy aerobic work will do more for you.

Six weighted hill sessions on Saturdays from 16 January, starting at 10 kg (about 11 percent of body weight at 90 kg) and reaching 22 kg (about 24 percent) for the overnight pack. The mountain weekend on 27 and 28 Feb supplies the big vertical that Hadleigh cannot. A garage version and the tyre sled cover bad weather.

**Cycling does not transfer well to the hills**Uphill Athlete coachesCycling keeps the heart and lungs fit but does not prepare you for descending or carrying load.

Their coaches describe athletes who trained for mountaineering mostly on the bike and struggled on the climb. The reasons given: you are seated, a smaller muscle mass is working, and there is no impact or posture work. One forum answer puts it at two to three times the cycling volume for the same effect, and a coach cites a Norwegian ski coach who counted bike time at one third. These are coaching opinions, not measured figures.

The bike counts in full for Tenerife. For the hills, Saturday on foot and Thursday step work run every week, and Phase 3 adds slow step-downs and loaded descents.

**Strength is a general circuit first, then heavy and simple, with the loaded step-up as the priority lift**Uphill AthleteTwo to three strength sessions a week through base, dropping to one or two for maintenance as the objective approaches.

Their general strength routine is an eight-exercise circuit: Turkish get-up, push-up, split bench squat, dips, box step-up, pull-up, squat, hanging leg raise. Move between exercises within 30 seconds and rest three minutes between laps. Start at one lap and add a lap every two weeks. They single out the loaded box step-up as the one exercise to keep if you keep only one. Masters sources add that strength work matters more with age, to slow muscle loss.

Tuesday starts as that circuit with your bells, then becomes a heavier single-leg session in Phase 2. I run the full circuit once a week with a lighter Thursday session, where Uphill Athlete would run it twice, to leave room for the bike build.

**MTNTOUGH is pack-and-bell conditioning with a mental toughness emphasis**MTNTOUGHBuilt for backcountry hunters and military users. Short coached sessions, minimal kit, lots of step-ups and loaded carries.

Their Heavy Pack 20 is a four-week follow-along programme needing a pack, sandbags, a box and a pull-up bar, with suggested starting pack weights of 30 to 40 lb for men. Foundation is a four-week entry programme of 30 to 40 minute sessions. KB20 is four weeks with a single kettlebell. Most programmes run five sessions a week and lean on timed circuits and AMRAPs.

That matches the two kettlebell workouts you already use. The conflict is frequency: five demanding circuits a week does not fit two hard days a week at 50, and their published material gives little on aerobic base or periodisation.

I took the equipment approach (pack, bell, box, carries) and the pack circuit as a bad-weather replacement for a hard day. I did not take the five-day circuit schedule.

**Set zones from a heart rate drift test, not a formula**Uphill AthleteHold a steady easy effort for an hour. If heart rate rises less than 5 percent from first half to second half, you started at or below aerobic threshold.

Reading the result: 3 to 5 percent drift means your starting heart rate was your aerobic threshold. Under 3 percent, repeat about 5 beats higher. Over 5 percent, repeat lower. Do it on a flat course or indoors, after a 15 minute warm-up. They report it agrees closely with lab testing.

Wednesday of Week 4, on the turbo at constant power. Your current Zone 2 runs 130 to 158, which is a wide band. Until the test, I suggest staying at 130 to 140 for easy work. That interim cap is my judgement, not from the sources.

**A drag sled fits, as part of the two hard days**Norrøna expedition school / sled researchDragging has no lowering phase, so it loads the legs hard with less soreness than squats or lunges. Tyre hauling is also the standard training for pulling a pulk.

Norrøna's expedition school describes the usual set-up: holes drilled in the tyre, an eye bolt or rope to a carabiner, 2.5 to 3 m of 6 to 7 mm static rope, and a pulk harness or a rucksack with a hip belt so the pull comes from the hips. They recommend poles, starting light, and adding tyres or gradient over time.

The low-soreness point is the common coaching reasoning. One published study (West and colleagues, 2014) measured recovery after backward sled drags, but I could only see its listing and not its results, so treat that claim as unverified.

Two limits. Dragging does not train the lowering strength that descents need, so the slow step-downs stay. And it is not a third hard day.

From Week 6, heavy drags replace the suitcase carry on Tuesday. A long steady drag is the second bad-weather option for Saturday. A light rope pull can replace rows on Thursday.

## Lake District Weekend

Two options for each day: a high route for settled conditions and a lower one for hard snow, ice or strong summit winds. My default is the high route on Saturday and the lower route on Sunday. Doing both high routes is about 1,650 m of climbing with 15 kg, which is a lot for the first big hill weekend in 20 years.

Saturday · high

### Skiddaw by the Jenkin Hill Path

From Keswick · about 16 km · about 950 m · 6 h · summit 931 m

Out and back on a broad path with simple navigation, which is why it suits a first winter outing. It is fully exposed to wind from Jenkin Hill upward. Starting from the Gale Road car park behind Latrigg cuts it to about 10 km and 700 m. Turn back at Little Man if the wind or the surface is beyond what you want that day.

Saturday · lower

### High Spy, Maiden Moor and Cat Bells

From Brandelhow or Hawse End · 14.6 km · 800 m · 5 to 6 h · top 653 m

Up Tongue Gill from Grange to High Spy, then north along the ridge over Maiden Moor to Cat Bells, and back along the lake. Nearly the same climbing as Skiddaw but 280 m lower, so it often stays below the snow line. The Keswick launch runs a reduced winter timetable, so check it or drive round to Hawse End.

Sunday · high

### Blencathra by Blease Fell

From Threlkeld · about 8 km · about 700 m · 4 h · summit 868 m

Up and down the broad western ridge. This is the walker's route. Leave Sharp Edge and Hall's Fell alone in winter; both are scrambles on a narrow crest. Starting from the Blencathra Centre car park saves some climbing.

Sunday · lower

### Walla Crag, Bleaberry Fell and Ashness Bridge

From Great Wood car park · 9.1 km · 544 m · about 4 h · top 586 m

A steep start past Lady's Rake to Walla Crag, across to Bleaberry Fell, then down by Falcon Crag to Ashness Bridge and back through the woods. Short enough to be home at a sensible hour, with enough descent to test tired legs.

**Figures.** The High Spy and Walla Crag numbers are from published route guides. The Skiddaw and Blencathra numbers are my own approximations, so measure them on a map before you rely on them. Times allow for a 15 kg pack.

Saturday · Phase 3

### Weighted Hill Session

Steepest climb you can find · 20 → 28 min of climbing · 10 → 22 kg

1. Walk in 20 min, unhurried
2. Climb at leg-burn effortLegs working hard, breathing still allows full sentences. Heart rate will read lower than the effort feels
3. Walk down easy, repeatCount only the uphill minutes toward the target
4. Walk out 15 min

Carry the weight as water. From 19 kg, tip half of it out at the top and refill at the bottom unless the descents feel comfortable. If the burn fades, the pack is too light or the slope too shallow.

Thursday · and bad-weather Saturday

### Step Work

30 cm box or bottom stair · 8 to 10 kg pack

**Thursday aerobic steps:** 25 to 40 minutes continuous, brisk, heart rate under aerobic threshold. Change the lead leg every minute.

**Saturday garage version:** Uphill Athlete's at-home muscular endurance workout. Box at about three quarters of the height to the bottom of your kneecap. Start with 4 sets of 10 step-ups per leg with 30 seconds rest, bodyweight only for the first two or three sessions. The full exercise list and progression are in their PDF, linked in the sources.

**Sled version:** harness and poles, a load you can keep moving, 20 m laps with a turn at each end. Use the same climbing minutes as that week's hill session, in blocks of 8 to 10 minutes with 2 minutes easy walking between.

**Pack circuit option:** your 15-minute AMRAP or 10-station circuit can stand in for a hard day when no hill session is possible. It never gets added as a third hard day.

Tests and rules

## How to Run It

| Test | When | How | What it decides |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Heart rate drift | Wed 4 Nov | Turbo. 15 min warm-up, then 60 min at one fixed power. Compare average heart rate of the two halves. | Your aerobic threshold, and the top of every easy session after it. |
| FTP | Tue 8 Dec | Your usual protocol, on the Assioma pedals. Note the average heart rate of the hard effort. | Power targets for pacing Teide. |
| Readiness for weighted hills | Week of 4 Jan | Compare aerobic threshold heart rate with the FTP test heart rate. I am using the FTP figure as a stand-in for anaerobic threshold. | Within 10 percent: start weighted sessions on 16 Jan. Wider: keep Saturdays as easy hill rucks for two more weeks. |

**Two hard days, no third**

Tuesday and Saturday. If one is missed, move it 48 to 72 hours and shift the other to keep the gap. Never stack them to catch up.

**Morning check**

Look at Garmin HRV status, resting heart rate against your usual 60, and Body Battery. If two of the three are off, swap that day's hard session for the easy version or rest.

**Hold before you push**

If a hard session starts with legs still tired from the last one, repeat the current week's numbers next week. The gap between hard days is the part of this plan that should not bend.

**Weight or distance, never both**

On Saturdays, a week that adds load keeps the same time, and a week that adds time keeps the same load.

**Easy means easy**

Five days a week you should finish feeling you could go again. Easy sessions that creep into Zone 3 are the junk volume you asked to avoid.

**The mountain weekend is three hard days in a week**

It breaks the two-hard-days rule on purpose, once, to rehearse consecutive days. Tuesday is lightened before it and Week 21 is a recovery week after it. Late February can be full winter conditions on the Lake District fells. Read the Weatherline fell top conditions report before each day and pick routes inside your current skills.

**Fitness is not winter skill**

This plan covers legs and lungs. Ice axe, crampons, winter navigation and avalanche awareness after 20 years away need a skills course or a guided day before you rely on them.

Build 1

### Step Box

18 mm structural plywood · glued and screwed · two working heights

Laid one way it is 30 cm high for aerobic steps. Turned on its side it is three quarters of your knee height, which is the Uphill Athlete height for the muscular endurance workout. Measure from the floor to the bottom of your kneecap, standing, in bare feet.

Floor to bottom of kneecap (cm)47 is an example, not your measurement.

Glue every joint and screw at 10 cm spacing with 4 × 40 mm screws into pre-drilled holes. Fit the brace across the middle of the 50 cm length. Round the edges and corners with a sander, because shins find them. A 1220 × 1220 mm half sheet covers it. Stand it on a rubber mat so it cannot slide.

Build 2

### Tyre Sled

Drag only · tarmac · rope and hip harness

1. One used car tyreTyre fitters usually give them away. Rubber grips tarmac hard, so start with the tyre empty
2. M10 eye bolt through the treadLarge washer inside and out, locking nut. Or thread the rope through two drilled holes and knot it
3. Plywood floor inside the tyreOffcut from the box. Cut a disc to sit on the lower sidewall, saw it in half to get it in, and join the halves with a batten. It holds a sandbag or a bell
4. 3 m of 6 to 7 mm static rope and a carabinerLong enough that the tyre stays clear of your heels
5. A second rope of 15 to 20 mFor hand-over-hand pulls. 10 to 12 mm is easier on the hands
6. HarnessA pulk harness, or an old rucksack with a proper hip belt. The pull should come from the hips, never the shoulders alone
7. Drain holes in the lower sidewall

Add load in small steps: an 8 kg bell, then 12, then a sandbag. A tyre is quieter than a timber or steel sled on tarmac but it will leave marks and wear, so use a surface where that is acceptable.

A gym membership or barbell is not needed for this plan. It becomes worth considering only if you outgrow the 24 kg bells on single-leg work.
